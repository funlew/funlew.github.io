几乎每个团队都有一套写在文档里的规则，也几乎每个团队都有两套实际执行中的规则。

一位做交付的朋友跟我抱怨：团队里最头疼的不是能力问题，而是「同一件事，两个人做，两种评价」。同一个漏测，一位被提醒下次注意，另一位被写进季度考核；同一个延期，一个项目被追责，另一个项目被归因为「需求变更」。他说这不是偏心——「情况确实不一样」。

我认同他最后半句，也认为前半句是个问题。这两个判断可以同时成立：**不一致往往不是有人使坏，而是没人负责让它一致**。

这篇文章想拆开三件事：一致性到底指什么、裂缝通常从哪里开始、以及怎么把它做成机制而不是口号。

<!-- more -->

## 一、先把词拆开：一致 ≠ 统一 ≠ 公平

这三个词在日常对话里几乎互换使用，但它们回答的是不同的问题：

| 概念 | 它回答的问题 | 失效时的样子 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 统一 | 大家是不是做一样的事 | 一刀切，牺牲情境差异 |
| 公平 | 付出与回报是否匹配 | 干多干少一个样，或者按关系分配 |
| 一致 | 同样的情况是否得到同样的对待，且能被预判 | 规则随人、随时、随情绪漂移 |

一致性买到的其实是**可预测性**。团队成员每次行动前都会做一次隐含预测：我这样做会被认可吗？我报风险会被追责吗？我说「做不到」会被尊重吗？预测稳定，人才敢投入；预测漂移，人就会去做两件更贵的事——**揣测**和**留证**。

> 一个开始在群里截图存档的人，通常不是想搞政治，只是不确定明天规则还在不在。

## 二、裂缝通常出现在五个地方

### 1. 目标一致性：指标先打架，然后才轮到人

| 场景 | A 的目标 | B 的目标 | 冲突落点 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 折扣签单 | 销售：本月回款 | 交付：毛利与人力 | 项目经理 |
| 尽快上线 | 产品：按期发布 | 安全：上线前完成评估 | 评审会 |
| 控制成本 | 财务：压缩预算 | 技术：稳定性投入 | 值班工程师 |

目标冲突本身不致命，致命的是**没有人为「冲突怎么裁决」负责**。冲突不会停在管理层，它会沿着层级往下沉，最后落在没有权限解决它的人身上，变成一句「你到底听谁的」。

判断信号很直接：两个岗位的 KPI 在同一个场景下无法同时满足，而规则里没写谁优先。

### 2. 标准一致性：同错不同罚，绩效体系当场失效

绩效从来不是绝对测量，而是相对比较。一旦标准随主管浮动，员工的最优策略就会从「把事做好」变成「跟对主管」——这不是员工的问题，是激励结构发出的信号。

修复方式不是把标准写得更细，而是做校准：不同主管先各自评估，再拿具体案例对齐「什么算达标」。校准的产物应该是**判例**，不是分数。分数会过期，判例能复用。

### 3. 决策一致性：同类问题每月重开一次

上个月定了「对外接口必须加鉴权」，这个月为了赶进度批了例外，下个月另一个团队来问「为什么他们可以」。三次会议，同一个问题，三种结论。

「特殊情况」是组织里最贵的四个字：它意味着重新谈判、重新承担风险、重新向所有人解释。更麻烦的是，每次重开都在暗示「规则可以谈」，于是下一次谈判的起点会更低。

修复工具简单到容易被低估：一份决策日志，三列就够——**场景、结论、例外条件**。它的作用不是留痕，而是让下一个遇到同类问题的人不必再开一次会。

### 4. 言行一致性：制定规则的人，往往是最容易豁免的人

要求九点同步，自己九点二十到；要求 PR 必须评审，自己的改动直接合并；要求复盘对事不对人，自己在复盘会上先定了性。

规则的执行力约等于两件事的乘积：**惩罚力度 × 被相信的程度**。前者靠职权维持，后者只能靠示范。当管理者自己成为例外，规则就退化成「上面的要求」，执行开始依赖监督强度——而监督强度有上限，示范没有。

成本最低的动作，是给自己加一条比团队更严的约束，并且公开它。

### 5. 信息一致性：同一个项目，三个版本的事实

对客户说「进展顺利」，对内说「有风险」，对自己说「快撑不住了」。三种口径未必是撒谎，多数时候只是对不同听众做了不同的裁剪。

问题不在裁剪，而在**裁剪没有记录**。等到要复盘时，没人能还原当时的真实判断，于是只能争论「当时到底怎么说的」，而不是「当时为什么这么判断」。

修复方式是单一事实来源：目标、排期、状态、接口人各自只有一个地址，所有对外版本从它派生，而不是各自维护一份。

## 三、为什么它总是复发

不一致几乎不是一次性事故，而是结构性产物。常见根因可以列成一张表：

| 根因 | 表象 | 对应动作 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 例外没有正式出口 | 所有例外都走特批，特批多了就成了新规则 | 定义例外条件、审批人与记录方式 |
| 规则没有 owner | 三年前的制度还在生效，没人说得清为什么 | 每条规则写清维护人与复审时间 |
| 管理者回避冲突 | 「这次情况特殊」比「不行」更容易说出口 | 把裁决权交给规则，而不是交给自己 |
| 组织扩张快于规则沉淀 | 十个人靠默契，五十个人靠猜 | 人数翻倍时重写一次协作约定 |
| 指标由不同部门定义 | 每个部门都达标，整体却失控 | 指定一个人对「指标是否打架」负责 |

还有一层不容易被说破的原因：不一致是有隐性收益的。它让管理者保留了自由裁量权，让「这次通融一下」成为人情，让难做的决定可以往后推。所以它不会自动消失，只会被机制替代——**你不设计规则，就会一直靠临场发挥维持秩序**。

## 四、六个动作，把一致性做成机制

1. **给例外一个出口。** 例外本身不是问题，隐形例外才是。规则里写清楚：什么条件可以破例、谁有权批、批完记在哪里。
2. **单一事实来源。** 目标、排期、状态各只有一个地址；汇报版本从它派生，而不是各写一份。
3. **把决策变成判例。** 决策日志按季度回看：哪些例外后来变成了常态？如果是，就改规则，而不是继续批。
4. **标准前置。** 先定义评价维度，再看具体的人；跨团队用校准会对齐案例。
5. **给规则一个版本号。** 每条规则有维护人、生效日期、复审日期。过期规则比没有规则更伤信任——因为它证明了「写下来的东西可以不作数」。
6. **管理者先约束自己。** 成本最低、见效最快，也最难装出来的一条。

## 五、一致性也有边界

反直觉的部分在这里：**过度一致比不一致更危险**。

- **阶段差异**：探索期需要容错，稳定期需要收敛。同一套流程套在两者身上，要么拖死探索，要么放过稳定期的风险。
- **能力差异**：新人需要更密的检查点，老手需要更大的授权。把两者拉平，成本由最贵的那些人承担。
- **情境差异**：事故期间可以集权，但战时状态不能变成常态。

判断一次差异是否破坏一致性，有三条标准，**全部满足才算健康**：

- **事先声明**：差异写在规则里，不是临场决定。
- **对所有人开放**：满足条件的人都能拿到同样的待遇。
- **可被解释**：给得出理由，而且这个理由不依赖某个人在场。

一句话概括：**允许规则不同，但获取规则的路径必须相同。**

## 六、一份自检清单

- [ ] 团队里有没有一条「大家都这么做，但没人写得出来」的规则？
- [ ] 上一次破例，是因为情况真的特殊，还是因为不想起冲突？
- [ ] 有没有两个岗位的 KPI 在同一场景下互相打架？谁负责裁决？
- [ ] 同类型的决策，有没有留下可检索的记录？
- [ ] 绩效标准是事先定义，还是事后解释？
- [ ] 最近三个月，管理者自己破过几次自己定的规矩？
- [ ] 团队规模翻倍后，协作约定重写过吗？
- [ ] 对内对外沟通的版本，是否都来自同一个事实源？
- [ ] 现有规则里，有多少条已经没人说得清为什么存在？
- [ ] 如果明天换一个人来做同样的决定，结论会一样吗？

最后一个问题最狠，因为它直接给了一致性的操作定义：**换个人来做，结论依然相同。**

## 七、结语

一致性不是让团队变整齐，而是让团队敢下注。当成员确信「这样做会被认可」「报风险不会被追责」，他们才会把注意力放在事情上，而不是放在揣摩上。

制度的价值不在写得漂亮，而在**每一次执行都在降低下一次执行的成本**。反过来，每一次没有记录的例外，都是在向组织收利息。

> 文中涉及的「程序公平」与「心理契约」是组织行为学的成熟概念：前者关注决策过程是否一致、是否可申诉（Greenberg 的程序公平研究），后者关注员工对互惠义务的隐性预期（Rousseau）。它们解释的是同一件事——人对一致性的敏感，往往高于对结果高低的敏感。

Almost every team has one set of rules written down in a document, and almost every team has another two sets actually in use.

A friend of mine who runs delivery complained about the thing that bothers him most: not capability, but "the same thing, done by two people, gets two verdicts." The same missed test case got one engineer a quiet reminder and another a line in their quarterly review. The same delay got one project blamed and another explained away as "requirements changed." He insisted it wasn't favoritism — "the situations really were different."

I agree with his last sentence, and I still think the first one is a problem. Both can be true at once: **inconsistency is rarely someone being malicious; it is usually nobody being responsible for making things consistent.**

This post tries to pull apart three things: what consistency actually means, where the cracks usually start, and how to turn it into a mechanism instead of a slogan.

<!-- more -->

## 1. Untangling the Words: Consistency Is Neither Uniformity nor Fairness

In everyday conversation the three words are used almost interchangeably, but they answer different questions:

| Concept | The question it answers | What it looks like when it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniformity | Is everyone doing the same thing? | One-size-fits-all, at the cost of context |
| Fairness | Do effort and reward match? | Everyone paid the same, or paid by relationship |
| Consistency | Does the same situation get the same treatment, predictably? | Rules drift with the person, the day, the mood |

What consistency actually buys is **predictability**. Before every action, a team member runs an implicit forecast: will this be recognized? Will reporting a risk get me blamed? Will saying "this isn't feasible" be respected? When the forecast is stable, people commit. When it drifts, people spend their energy on two far more expensive activities — **guessing** and **keeping receipts**.

> Someone who starts screenshotting conversations is usually not playing politics. They just don't know whether tomorrow's rules will still be today's.

## 2. Five Places the Cracks Appear

### 1. Goal consistency: the metrics fight first, then the people

| Situation | Goal A | Goal B | Where the conflict lands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discount to close | Sales: cash this month | Delivery: margin and headcount | The project manager |
| Ship sooner | Product: hit the date | Security: complete the review first | The launch review |
| Cut cost | Finance: lower budget | Engineering: reliability work | The on-call engineer |

Goal conflict is not fatal by itself. What is fatal is **nobody owning the arbitration**. The conflict doesn't stay at the management layer; it sinks down the hierarchy until it reaches someone with no authority to resolve it, and turns into "so whose priority is it anyway?"

The signal is simple: two roles' KPIs cannot both be satisfied in the same situation, and no rule says which one wins.

### 2. Standard consistency: unequal punishment for equal mistakes kills the review process

Performance review is never an absolute measurement; it is a comparison. The moment the standard floats with the manager, the employee's best strategy shifts from "do the work well" to "pick the right manager." That is not a character flaw — it is what the incentive structure is telling them.

The fix is not a longer rubric. It is calibration: managers assess independently first, then align on concrete cases of what "meets the bar" looks like. The output should be **precedents**, not scores. Scores expire; precedents get reused.

### 3. Decision consistency: the same question reopens every month

Last month the rule was "every external interface needs authentication." This month an exception was approved to hit a deadline. Next month another team asks why that team was allowed to skip it. Three meetings, one question, three conclusions.

"This case is special" is the most expensive phrase in an organization: it means renegotiating, re-taking the risk, and re-explaining to everyone. Worse, every reopening signals that the rule is negotiable, so the next negotiation starts from a lower baseline.

The tool is simple enough to be underestimated: a decision log with three columns — **situation, ruling, exception conditions**. Its purpose isn't documentation. It's saving the next person from having to call another meeting.

### 4. Word–action consistency: the people who write the rules are the easiest to exempt

The standup is at nine, but they arrive at 9:20. Pull requests must be reviewed, but theirs get merged directly. Post-mortems are supposed to be blameless, but they assign blame in the first five minutes.

Rule enforcement is roughly the product of two things: **the severity of the consequence × how much people believe in the rule**. The first is held up by authority; the second only by example. Once a manager becomes the exception, the rule degrades into "something upstairs wants," and enforcement starts to depend on how closely people are watched — and supervision has a ceiling. Example doesn't.

The cheapest move available: give yourself a stricter constraint than the team has, and make it public.

### 5. Information consistency: one project, three versions of the facts

"On track" to the client, "some risk" internally, "I'm barely holding this together" to yourself. Three versions are not necessarily lies; most of the time they are the same truth trimmed for different audiences.

The trimming isn't the problem. **Untracked trimming** is. When it's time to look back, nobody can reconstruct what was actually believed, so the argument becomes "what did we say at the time" instead of "why did we judge it that way."

The fix is a single source of truth: one address each for goals, schedule, status and the point of contact. Every external version is derived from it rather than maintained separately.

## 3. Why It Keeps Coming Back

Inconsistency is almost never a one-off accident; it is a structural product. The usual root causes fit in one table:

| Root cause | Symptom | Move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No formal channel for exceptions | Every exception needs a special approval, and enough special approvals become the new rule | Define the conditions, the approver and the record |
| Rules have no owner | A policy from three years ago is still in force and nobody can say why | Name a maintainer and a review date for each rule |
| Managers avoid conflict | "This case is special" is easier to say than "no" | Give the ruling to the rule, not to yourself |
| Growth outpaces codification | Ten people run on rapport, fifty people run on guesswork | Rewrite the working agreement whenever headcount doubles |
| Metrics defined by separate departments | Every department hits target while the whole drifts | Name one person accountable for whether the metrics conflict |

There is also a reason people rarely say out loud: inconsistency has hidden benefits. It preserves discretion, turns "let it slide this time" into a favor, and lets hard decisions be postponed. So it will not disappear on its own. It only gets replaced by a mechanism — **if you don't design rules, you will spend your days improvising order.**

## 4. Six Moves That Turn Consistency Into a Mechanism

1. **Give exceptions a channel.** Exceptions aren't the problem; invisible exceptions are. Write down what qualifies, who can approve it, and where the approval is recorded.
2. **One source of truth.** Goals, schedule and status each live in exactly one place. Reports are derived from it, not maintained in parallel.
3. **Turn decisions into precedents.** Review the decision log quarterly: which exceptions quietly became the norm? If so, change the rule instead of approving more of them.
4. **Define the standard first.** Agree on the dimensions before looking at the person; use calibration sessions across teams to align on cases.
5. **Version your rules.** Every rule gets a maintainer, an effective date and a review date. An expired rule damages trust more than no rule at all — it proves that what was written down can stop counting.
6. **Constraint yourself first.** The cheapest, fastest and hardest to fake of the six.

## 5. Consistency Has Limits

Here is the counterintuitive part: **too much consistency is more dangerous than too little.**

- **Stage differences**: exploration needs tolerance for error; stability needs convergence. Applying one process to both either strangles exploration or waves through risk that a stable system can't absorb.
- **Skill differences**: newcomers need tighter checkpoints; veterans need wider authority. Flatten the two and the cost lands on your most expensive people.
- **Context differences**: centralizing during an incident is right; wartime mode becoming the default is not.

Three tests tell you whether a difference breaks consistency. **All three must hold**:

- **Declared in advance**: it lives in the rules, not in the moment.
- **Open to everyone**: anyone who meets the condition gets the same treatment.
- **Explainable**: there is a reason, and the reason doesn't depend on who is in the room.

In one line: **rules may differ, but the path to the rule must be the same.**

## 6. A Self-Check List

- [ ] Is there a rule everyone follows but nobody can write down?
- [ ] Was the last exception genuinely special, or just easier than a conflict?
- [ ] Do two roles' KPIs conflict in the same situation, and who arbitrates?
- [ ] Do decisions of the same type leave a searchable record?
- [ ] Is the performance standard defined up front or explained afterwards?
- [ ] How many of your own rules has the manager broken in the last three months?
- [ ] Has the working agreement been rewritten since the team doubled?
- [ ] Do internal and external versions both come from the same source of truth?
- [ ] How many current rules can no longer be explained by anyone?
- [ ] If someone else made the same decision tomorrow, would the conclusion be the same?

That last question is the harshest, because it hands you an operational definition: **same decision, different person, same conclusion.**

## 7. Closing

Consistency doesn't make a team tidy; it makes a team willing to bet. When people are confident that good work gets recognized and reported risk doesn't get punished, they put their attention on the work instead of on reading the room.

A system earns its value not by reading well, but by **making every execution cheaper than the last**. Every unrecorded exception, on the other hand, is interest charged to the organization.

> The two ideas referenced here — procedural justice and the psychological contract — are established concepts in organizational behavior. The first concerns whether decision processes are consistent and appealable (Greenberg's work on procedural justice); the second concerns employees' implicit expectations of reciprocal obligation (Rousseau). Both explain the same thing: people are often more sensitive to consistency than to the size of the outcome.
